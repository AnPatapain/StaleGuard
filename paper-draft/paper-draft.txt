[TOPIC/CONTEXT] Autonomous Unmanned Aircraft Systems (UAS) use hierarchical control architecture in which the Ground Control Station (GCS) sends high-level commands, such as mission goals to the Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) and the UAV's onboard controller turns them into motor signals. [MOTIVATING PHENOMENON] In practice, UAV commands are not always executed on reception: task scheduler may queue the received maneuver commands for later execution [1] or schedule newly received flight plans to execute after the current plan [2], ArduPilot also supports delaying mission actions while the UAV continues navigating [3]. As UAV continues moving, especially during fast-transient maneuvers, rapid change in its dynamic state and surrounding environment can make the safety margin that justified a command's approval decreases rapidly, potentially making it unsafe at dispatch time. [EXAMPLE] An inspection descent mission, for example, may be safe when approved by ground-station but unsafe after UAV approaches an unsafe zone. That leads us to a research question: How should UAS handles such commands when the dispatch delays onboard is unknown?

[CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND] This problem is similar to the time-to-check to time-to-use (TOC/TOU), a classic race condition bug in software-engineering. Shraga et al. [4] formalize stale safety approvals in self-adaptive systems and estimate their validity horizons without a dynamics model. Lu et al. [5] addressed stale UAV commands through timing and state-consistency check at dispatch time. Beyond command-level validation, Althoff et al.[6] uses online reachability analysis to verify planned UAV trajectories under state uncertainty and disturbances, while preserving safe fallback options. Predictive safety filters assess proposed actions through future-state prediction. Black-Box Simplex [7] uses predictive monitoring to trigger backup control, while AESOP [8] prepares fallback trajectories during high-level reasoning. [RESEARCH GAP FROM THEM] Yet these studies do not specifically characterize how unknown dispatch delays affect safety and mission completion when approved UAV commands remain pending during flight, or systematically quantify the resulting trade-offs among different safety runtime-assurance policies.

In this work, we show that delayed execution of approved UAV commands is not merely a validity problem: intervening too late can compromise flight safety, while intervening too early can sacrifice mission completion. We propose StaleGuard, an onboard safety runtime-assurance which uses lightweight closed-loop simulation to predict when a pending command becomes unsafe, allowing command-specific intervention without knowing its eventual dispatch delay. We compare StaleGuard with six other runtime-assurances mechanisms in two UAV missions: inspection descent and geofence-constrained turning. In the geofence-constrained turn experiment, with 13,800 simulated UAV flights, StaleGuard reduces unsafe flights by 68.9% over fixed-time early recovery, with a 0.12 percentage-point decrease in safe mission completion.

[MAIN TAKEAWAY] Our findings show that handling delayed commands in queue-based multi-UAV systems requires more than checking whether a command is safe at dispatch. It also matters when to start intervening and what mission progress should be sacrificed to stay safe. Predictive model-based method can support this decision, but only with accurate predictions and fast computation, which is not an easy task on limited hardware of UAV onboard companion computer. Our work gives an empirical basis for designing and evaluating time-sensitive safety runtime-assurance method in UAS mission software.

The main contributions of this work are:
- We characterize how unknown dispatch delays affect the safety and mission completion of previously approved UAV supervisory maneuvers across two safety-critical mission settings.

- We introduce StaleGuard, which estimates command-specific validity horizons using lightweight closed-loop simulation, and investigate how these estimates enable intervention before dispatch without advance knowledge of the delay.

- We compare multiple command-validation and recovery policies, quantify their safety–mission-completion trade-offs, analyze prediction-related failures, and develop an extensible simulation benchmark for evaluating delayed-command safety mechanisms.
